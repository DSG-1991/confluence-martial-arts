The Weaponry of Takeuchi Renshinkan

Takeuchi Renshinkan, founded in 1532, is one of the oldest documented Japanese martial systems. Unlike modern martial arts that were developed for sport or fitness, Takeuchi Renshinkan was designed for the battlefield. Weapons were not optional — they were essential. A samurai needed to be proficient with multiple weapons, and the system reflects that reality.

The weapons curriculum in Takeuchi Renshinkan includes:

Tachi (Long Sword)

The katana is the iconic weapon of the samurai, and training with the tachi forms a core part of the Takeuchi Renshinkan curriculum. Students learn cutting angles, footwork, distancing, and the principles of swordsmanship that underpin the entire system. Even if you never intend to wield a real blade, sword training develops a profound understanding of timing, spatial awareness, and body mechanics that transforms your empty-hand techniques.

Kodachi (Short Sword)

The short sword teaches a different set of principles — closer ranges, faster movements, and the ability to work in confined spaces. Training with the kodachi complements the long sword work and gives students a more complete understanding of blade dynamics.

Jo (Short Staff)

The jo is a versatile weapon that appears in many traditional Japanese martial arts. In Takeuchi Renshinkan, jo training develops fluidity, timing, and the ability to transition between offensive and defensive movements seamlessly. The jo is also an excellent introduction to weapons work for beginners, as its length and weight make it accessible while still teaching fundamental principles.

The Six and a Half Point Pole (六點半棍)

This is a weapon that holds special significance at Confluence Martial Arts. Craig Abernethy trained the six and a half point pole directly with Guru Dan Inosanto at the Inosanto Academy in Marina del Rey, California. This pole technique is a sophisticated weapon form that develops power generation, distance management, and fluid transitions. It is a rare skill in the UK, and Craig's training in it brings a unique dimension to the weapons curriculum at Confluence.

How Weapons Training Complements Empty-Hand Jujutsu

One of the most common misconceptions about weapons training is that it is separate from empty-hand martial arts. In the traditional Japanese context, the two are inseparable. Here is why weapons training makes you a better martial artist overall:

Distance and Timing

When you train with a weapon, your understanding of distance changes fundamentally. A sword extends your reach by several feet, and the timing of an attack with a blade is completely different from a punch or a kick. Training with weapons sharpens your sense of ma-ai (combat distance) in a way that empty-hand practice alone cannot achieve. This heightened awareness carries directly into your jujutsu, making your distance management more precise and intuitive.

Body Mechanics

Weapons training demands efficient body mechanics. A sword cut that originates from the arms alone is weak; a proper cut comes from the hips, core, and whole body working together. The same principle applies to jujutsu techniques — a throw or a lock is far more effective when the whole body is engaged. Weapons work teaches you to move as a unified whole, and that integration transforms your empty-hand technique.

Understanding Attack and Defence

When you have trained against a weapon — even a wooden practice weapon — you develop a visceral understanding of what a real attack looks like. You learn to read intent, to move off the line of attack, and to recognise the moments when an opponent is vulnerable. This awareness is invaluable for self-defence, where attacks can come from any angle and at any speed.

Who Is Weapons Training For?

Weapons training at Confluence Martial Arts is open to all students who are ready to expand their practice. You do not need any prior weapons experience — everything is taught progressively, starting with basic grips, stances, and movements before moving on to more complex forms and applications.

Weapons work is particularly rewarding for:

  • Martial artists who want to deepen their understanding of traditional Japanese combat systems
  • Jujutsu practitioners who want to see how weapon principles inform empty-hand techniques
  • History enthusiasts who want to connect with the living traditions of feudal Japan
  • Anyone looking for a new challenge that engages both mind and body
